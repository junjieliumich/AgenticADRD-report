Misperceived Social Norms: Women Working Outside the Home in Saudi Arabia

A structured comparison of four study components reported by Bursztyn, González, and Yanagizawa-Drott (American Economic Review, 2020), with selectable evidence and explicit coverage limits.

Open source PDF

Study overview

The paper studies whether Saudi men underestimate support for women working outside the home (WWOH), whether correcting perceived norms changes household labor-supply behavior, and whether the same information changes women’s choices in an actual recruitment process. The comparison separates the main experiment, a national list experiment, a later randomized wording check, and the surveyor recruitment field experiment.

Experiment summaries

P1-E1

Main Experiment

Riyadh field experiment with 500 married Saudi men aged 18–35, followed three to five months later by phone. Individual information treatment corrected beliefs about session peers’ support for WWOH.

P1-E2

First National Survey · list experiment

National online survey of married Saudi men aged 18–35. Random assignment to three-item control or four-item list elicited support for WWOH with plausible deniability.

P1-E3

Second National Survey · randomized wording check

The paper calls this a “separate online survey” conducted in January–February 2020. It randomized the wording of the WWOH opinion question and also measured discussion frequency and perceived support.

P1-E4

Surveyor Recruitment Experiment

Natural recruitment experiment among 291 women in three cities. Before choosing between at-home and higher-paid outside-home enumerator work, half heard national-survey norm information.

Unresolved gaps and limitations

  • The supplied PDF does not contain the online appendix it cites. Appendix-only tables, figures, scripts, and model material cannot be reviewed here.
  • The second national survey reports 703 respondents overall and percentages for the 18–35 subgroup, but the subgroup denominator is not stated in the PDF.
  • For several experiments, assignment implementation details, missing-data procedures, or interference assumptions are not stated; these are recorded as coverage gaps rather than assumed.
  • The paper describes the first national survey’s final sample as “about 1,500” and reports 1,496 launched after a 100-person pilot, of whom 1,460 completed the whole survey. The analysis says the pilot was dropped, but exact outcome-specific denominators are not printed in the main text.
